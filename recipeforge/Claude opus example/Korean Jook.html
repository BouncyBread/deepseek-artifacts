Using ¾ cup uncooked jasmine rice · makes about 4 bowls

In Korea, when you’re sick or tired or just need to feel held, someone makes you 죽. This is the homestyle chicken version — rice toasted in fragrant sesame oil until it smells nutty, then simmered slowly with chicken, lots of garlic, and a thumb of ginger until the grains practically melt into a creamy hug. Your Cuckoo does almost all the work.

A note on jasmine: traditional dakjuk uses short-grain rice, which gets creamier. Jasmine works beautifully too — slightly less sticky, a touch more fragrant. If you have both, mix them.

Method 조리법

1

Rinse and soak the rice

Put your jasmine rice in a bowl. Cover with cool water, swish gently with your hand for about 10 seconds, and drain. Repeat 3 times total. The water won’t go fully clear — and you don’t want it to. That cloudiness is starch, and starch is what makes porridge feel like porridge.

Cover the rinsed rice with fresh cool water and let it soak for 30 minutes while you prep everything else. (Skipping the soak is fine; the rice just takes longer to break down.)

Don’t over-rinse — the cloudy starch is what makes the porridge silky.
2

Prep the aromatics and vegetables

While the rice soaks, do all your chopping. Smash and roughly chop 4 of the garlic cloves; thinly slice the other half and set them aside in a separate pile. Peel and thinly slice the ginger.

The carrot, onion, and zucchini all need to be cut very small — finer than you think. They should disappear into the porridge, not stand out as chunks. Aim for pieces about the size of a grain of cooked rice.

Tiny dice = better porridge. If your knife skills aren’t there yet, pulse the veg in a food processor a few times.
3

Toast the rice in sesame oil

This is the one stove step, and it’s the secret to that nutty, restaurant-quality flavor. Drain your soaked rice well. Heat the toasted sesame oil in a small skillet over medium-low heat (sesame oil burns fast — keep the heat gentle).

Add the drained rice along with the chopped garlic and the sliced ginger. Stir constantly for about 2 minutes, until the rice grains turn slightly translucent at the edges and smell deeply nutty and toasty. Don’t walk away.

Why this matters Toasting in sesame oil before adding liquid is what separates homemade-Korean-grandma juk from generic rice cooker congee. Don’t skip it.
4

Load the Cuckoo

Scrape everything from the skillet (oil, rice, aromatics) into the Cuckoo’s inner pot. Add the raw chicken thighs, the remaining sliced garlic, the diced carrot, onion, and zucchini, the salt, and finally the broth.

Give it one gentle stir to settle everything. Don’t overfill — the liquid should sit comfortably below your pot’s maximum porridge line. If it’s close, scoop out a little broth.

Important — do not overfill Porridge expands and bubbles a lot as it cooks. If your liquid sits above the porridge max line, it can spit out the steam vent or trigger the safety shutoff. If you’re over the line, switch to the stovetop method in step 5 — don’t try to muscle through it.
5

Cook it · pick your method

Method A · Cuckoo

Press Porridge. Walk away. Lock the lid, select Porridge (죽), press start. Depending on your model, this runs somewhere between 50 minutes and 1 hour 10 minutes. The cooker counts down and switches to keep-warm automatically.

This is your moment to make tea, fold laundry, or lie down if you’re sick — which, let’s be honest, is probably why you’re making this.

or
Method B · Stovetop

Use this when you’re cooking a bigger batch and the liquid would go above the Cuckoo’s porridge max line, or if you don’t have a Cuckoo at all. You can actually do steps 1–3 directly in the pot to save dishes.

Use a heavy-bottomed pot — a Dutch oven is ideal, otherwise any thick pot at least 5–6 quarts. The thicker the bottom, the less it’ll scorch.

  • Bring the loaded pot to a gentle boil over medium-high heat, then immediately drop to the lowest simmer your stove can manage.
  • Lid on, but cracked — leave a finger-width gap so steam can escape (a sealed lid will boil over).
  • Stir every 10–15 minutes, scraping the bottom each time so nothing sticks. This is the only real difference from the rice cooker version.
  • Cook 45–60 minutes total, until the rice has broken down and the porridge is creamy.
  • Keep a kettle of hot broth or water nearby. Stovetops evaporate more than a sealed Cuckoo — if it gets too thick before the rice is fully soft, stir in a splash. Always add it hot; cold water mid-cook makes the porridge gluey.
  • It should look soupy at the start — way more liquid than seems right. That’s correct. The rice swells and absorbs most of it as it breaks down.
Heavy pot · lowest simmer · lid cracked · stir every 10–15 min.
6

Shred the chicken & mash the garlic

When the cycle ends, carefully open the lid (lots of steam — lean back). Fish out the chicken thighs with tongs onto a plate. They’ll be very tender. Use two forks to shred them into small, bite-sized pieces.

Find the soft, mellow cooked garlic cloves still in the pot and gently mash them against the side with the back of a spoon — they’ll dissolve into the porridge and add a sweet, deep garlic flavor without any harshness.

Stir the shredded chicken back in. Taste, and add more salt a pinch at a time until it tastes gently savory — not aggressive. Korean juk is meant to be soothing, not seasoned to death.

7

Check the consistency

The porridge should fall slowly off a spoon — neither soupy nor pasty. Use this as your guide:

Korean juk also thickens dramatically as it cools, so aim a touch thinner than your final goal. If you’re reheating leftovers tomorrow, you’ll definitely add water.

8

Bowl & finish

Ladle the hot porridge into deep bowls. Don’t fill them to the brim — leave room for the toppings, which are half the joy.

Top each bowl with a generous pinch of scallions, a sprinkle of toasted sesame seeds, a few crumbled or ribbon-cut sheets of gim, and a small drizzle of toasted sesame oil right at the end.

Serve immediately, with kimchi on the side and a little soy sauce at the table.

Add the sesame oil last, off the heat — heat destroys its perfume.

Pro tips 팁

·
Use thighs, not breast. The little bit of fat in thighs gives the broth that mouth-coating richness you can’t fake with breast.
·
Under-season at first. Korean juk is meant to taste mild and gentle — the toppings and kimchi at the table do the heavy seasoning. Add salt slowly.
·
Mixing rices? A 2:1 ratio of jasmine to short-grain (or sweet rice) gives you the creaminess of Korean juk plus the floral aroma of jasmine.
·
For abalone juk (jeonbokjuk): swap chicken for 1 thinly sliced abalone, added in step 4.
·
For babies/elderly: blend a portion smooth with extra broth. Skip the salt; let adults season their own bowls.

Storage 보관

·
Fridge: up to 3 days in an airtight container. It will set into a thick custard — totally normal.
·
Reheating: stovetop over low heat with a generous splash of water or broth, stirring constantly. Microwave works in a pinch — stir every 60 seconds.
·
Freezer: portion into individual containers; freezes well up to 2 months. Thaw in the fridge before reheating.
·
Don’t pre-top: only add scallions, sesame oil, sesame seeds, and gim to the bowl you’re eating now. They go limp in storage.
